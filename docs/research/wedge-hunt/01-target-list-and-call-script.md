# Target list and discovery script

## Who to call

**Profile.** Owner-operator, controller or office manager at a $3–30M oilfield-services supplier with 3–8 operator customers, 50–400 tickets a month, one or two billing staff, and Houston or Permian operations. Service lines where tickets are high-volume and low-value: water hauling, vacuum trucks, rental equipment, roustabout, hot-shot, chemicals, wireline, pressure testing, location services.

**Why this profile.** Enough tickets for the chase to be a daily job, not enough revenue for a dedicated AR team or FieldFX. These firms factor.

**Sources, in order of yield.**
1. Companies hiring for this loop (from week-one check 3). They have proven they pay for it.
2. Energy Workforce & Technology Council member directory, filtered to Houston service companies under 200 employees.
3. LinkedIn: title "billing coordinator" or "AR specialist" at companies whose description includes oilfield services, Houston. Call the owner, not the coordinator.
4. Factoring companies' published client case studies (TCI, RTS, Triumph, Scale Funding). A supplier in a factoring case study has the problem by definition.
5. Jay's own Houston network: anyone who knows an OFS owner. One warm intro beats five cold calls.

**Not these.** Majors' direct suppliers over $50M (they have FieldFX or a team), drilling contractors (different billing model), anyone whose main customer is a single operator (the chase is one relationship, not a loop).

## The call

This is discovery, not a pitch. The goal is to learn where the days go and whether the owner feels it. Twenty minutes. Do not describe a product; there is none.

**Open (30 seconds).**
"I'm Jay with Quiet Bands in Houston. I'm looking at how service companies get paid by operators, specifically the gap between a ticket getting signed and the money landing. I'm not selling anything today. Could I ask you about your last ticket that took more than 30 days to get paid?"

**The one question that matters.**
"Walk me through it. Where did the days go?"

Let them talk. Write down every step, every person, every system they name. Then follow the chain with these, in whatever order fits:

- "Who signs your tickets in the field? Paper or on a device? How often does a ticket come back unsigned or come back late?"
- "Which portals do you invoice through? How many logins does your billing person have?"
- "When an invoice gets disputed or rejected, who finds out, and how long after it happened?"
- "What's your DSO right now? What was it a year ago?"
- "Do you factor? What rate? Which invoices?"
- "If I took a snapshot of every open ticket today, how many would be waiting on a signature, how many on operator approval, how many approved but unpaid?"
- "Who does the chasing today? What else is that person supposed to be doing?"
- "Has anyone ever offered to do this for you? What happened?"
- "If someone owned that whole chase and you could see every ticket's status every Friday, what would that be worth to you a month? Say a number, even a rough one."

**The ask (last two minutes).**
"Two things, if you're open to it. First, could you send me an export of your open and recently paid tickets with the dates on them? Ticket date, submitted, approved, paid, operator. I'll send back a one-page read of where your days actually go, free, no strings. Second, if the read shows something worth fixing, would you let us work your queue for two weeks under your logins so we can prove it moves?"

**Close.** Confirm who sends the export and by when. Put it in the list.

## What to log after every call

Within ten minutes, in this file under the company's row or in a notes section:

- Where they said the days go, in their words.
- Which of the four stages they blamed: field signature, office submission, operator approval, payment terms.
- Portals named.
- DSO and factoring rate if given.
- The number they said when asked what it would be worth.
- Whether they will send the export (yes / maybe / no) and the date promised.
- Any sentence that sounded like a kill criterion ("operators won't let third parties in", "it's all net 60, nothing to chase", "our tickets just don't get signed").

## Objections you will hear

- **"Our billing girl handles it."** Ask what else she is supposed to be doing and how many portals she is in. Ask what happens when she is out for a week.
- **"The operators pay when they pay."** Ask what share of tickets are approved-but-unpaid versus never-approved. If it is nearly all the former, this company is a kill-criterion data point, not a prospect. Thank them and log it.
- **"We factor, it's handled."** Ask the rate. Then ask whether they would rather pay a third of that to get paid on the same timeline without selling the invoice.
- **"We tried software."** Ask which, and what it did and did not do. Log the vendor name.
- **"Who are you?"** "A Houston studio that solves back-office problems by hand first and only builds software for the ones that turn out to be real. That's why I'm asking, not pitching."

## Scheduling

Batch the calls into two afternoons. Eight on the first, seven on the second, fifteen-minute gaps. Do not spread them across the week.
