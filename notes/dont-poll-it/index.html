Don't poll it. Try to kill it.

Field note

Why asking too many people kills good ideas, and why we try to kill ours anyway.

Quiet Bands·October 2026·5 min read

There's a poster going around: “The fastest way to kill a good idea is asking too many people what they think about it.”

It's true.

It also sounds like the opposite of what we say.

Our line is “Before you build it, try to kill it.” It's the reason the bulb on our home page breaks.

So which is it? Protect the idea, or try to kill it?

Both.

The difference is what you use as the weapon.

01Opinions are cheap to give

Ask ten people what they think and you get ten answers. Most are polite. Some are confident. Almost none of them cost the person anything.

An opinion about an idea is a prediction about behavior, made by someone who won't have to behave.

  • “I'd use that.”
  • “Sounds cool.”
  • “I don't get it.”
  • “Someone's probably already doing this.”

Any one of those can kill an idea. None of them is evidence.

The crowd doesn't kill ideas because it's right. It kills them because it's loud, and because the average of enough opinions is almost always “maybe.”

Nobody builds on maybe. So the idea just stops.

Asking is not testing.

02Trying to kill it is a different question

“What do you think?” is a question about the idea.

Trying to kill it is a question about the world:

  • What goes wrong, for whom, and how often?
  • What do people do today instead?
  • What does it cost them?
  • What have you actually seen, as opposed to assumed?
  • Has anyone doing the work told you this themselves?

None of those ask whether anyone likes the idea.

They ask whether the problem is real.

You don't need anyone's approval to answer them. You need to look.

03The workaround is the vote that counts

The strongest evidence isn't what people say. It's what they already do.

  • A spreadsheet someone keeps up by hand.
  • A text thread that is quietly the real system.
  • A step that only exists because the software doesn't.

A workaround is someone paying for the problem with their time, every week, without being asked.

That's a vote. It was cast before you showed up, and nobody was being polite when they cast it.

The questions that shaped Frenchies didn't come from a survey about whether a coffee benefit was a good idea. They came from the counter. Who qualifies? Has it been used? What happens when someone wants a latte instead of a drip?

Opinions predict. Behavior reports.

04Talk to fewer people. Better ones.

The poster isn't against conversation. Neither are we. The Field Guide has a whole section on when research should stop and human conversations should start.

The difference is who you ask, and what.

Not: “Would you use this?”

But:

  • “Walk me through the last time this went wrong.”
  • “What did you do?”
  • “What did it cost you?”

Ask about the past, not the future. Ask the people doing the work, not the people standing near it.

A few of those conversations are worth more than a room full of opinions.

05Most ideas should die. Early, and on purpose.

Here's the uncomfortable part.

Building software got easier. Knowing what deserves to be built didn't.

When almost anything can be built over a weekend, the expensive mistake isn't failing to build. It's building the wrong thing well.

An idea killed by evidence in a week cost you a week. An idea killed by indifference after launch cost you the build, the launch, and some of your nerve for the next one.

That's why our Pressure Test is written so it can tell you not to build. Most ideas that come through it shouldn't be built yet. It ends on one of five decisions:

  • Research more.
  • Talk to humans.
  • Test manually.
  • Explore a build.
  • Park it.

Parking an idea isn't failure. It's a decision, made on purpose, with reasons you can come back to.

An idea that survives a real attempt on its life is worth more than one everybody liked.

06So: protect it, or kill it?

Protect it from opinions.

Expose it to evidence.

Don't ask too many people what they think. Ask the right few what they do.

Then try, honestly, to kill it.

If it's still standing, build it.

Before you build it, try to kill it.
