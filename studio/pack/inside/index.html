01

Part 01. Kill it before you generate it

Five questions that decide whether an idea is worth a single credit.

Credits are film stock. Most of them get spent finding out that an idea was never going to work, one generation at a time. Writing it down costs nothing. So before you open a generator, put the idea through five questions. They are written to be able to say no.

  1. One sentence. Can you say the scene with a person, a want and a problem in it? “A woman finds a key under her mat that isn’t hers.” If it takes a paragraph, it isn’t a scene yet.
  2. The first frame. What is on screen in the first two seconds? If you can’t picture it, the scroll wins before your story starts.
  3. The turn. Something has to change between the start and the end: a reveal, a choice, a reversal. If nothing changes, it’s a mood board.
  4. Small enough to hold. Can it be made with one or two characters and one or two locations? Every new face is a consistency problem. Every new location is another look to keep steady.
  5. Twice. Would you watch it twice? The ending should ask a question or pay one off.
ScoreCall
Five yesMake it.
Three or fourFix the weak answer on paper first, then make it.
Two or fewerPark it. Keep the note. Spend the credits on the next one.

02

Part 02. The 60-second drama

A beat sheet with timings, and a worked example you can steal the shape of.

Short drama works when every second has a job. Five beats, timed for 60 seconds. For a 30-second piece, halve each one and cut the second escalation.

TimeBeatIts job
0–3 sHookOpen mid-action, or on an image that asks a question. No logo, no title card, no “so…”.
3–12 sWho and wantOne person, one want. Shown, not said.
12–35 sThe wallThe problem gets worse twice. Escalate, don’t repeat.
35–50 sThe turnA reveal or a choice that flips what we thought was happening.
50–60 sThe cutEnd on a question, a look or a line. Cut a beat earlier than feels comfortable.

Plan eight to fourteen shots for a minute. Most video generators hand back clips of a few seconds, and the usable part of each is shorter than that, so a shot of four to six seconds on screen is a good unit to plan in.

Worked example: “The Spare Key”

Hook
Extreme close-up. A key taped under a doormat. A hand peels the tape back.
Who and want
Nia, late, arms full of groceries, just wants to get inside. She stops. Her own key is already in her hand. This one isn’t hers.
The wall
It opens her door anyway. Inside, a second toothbrush she doesn’t own. Her phone lights up: “Did you find it?” Unknown number.
The turn
Across the hall, her neighbour’s door is open an inch. Someone inside is watching.
The cut
Nia locks her door from the inside. Then she hears the key turn from the outside.

One sentence, one location, two faces, a turn and an ending that asks a question. It passes all five questions in part 01, which is why it is worth generating.

03

Part 03. Character lock

How to keep one face the same across every shot of an episode.

Faces drift between generations. The fix is not a longer prompt. It is deciding the character once, in words and in pictures, and then refusing to improvise.

The identity block

Fill this in once per character. Paste it into every prompt for that character, word for word, in the same order. Same words, same face.

NAME:
AGE RANGE:
SKIN TONE:
FACE: (shape, jaw, brows, one detail people would notice)
HAIR: (style, length, colour)
EYES:
BUILD:
MARKS: (scar, freckles, tattoo, glasses)
WARDROBE, THIS EPISODE:
ONE PROP:
Filled in
Nia, late 20s, deep brown skin, oval face with high cheekbones and a small gap in her front teeth, shoulder-length box braids with copper ends, dark brown eyes, slim build, small gold hoop in left ear only, oversized olive bomber jacket over a grey hoodie, a ring of keys on a red lanyard.

The rules

  1. Reference sheet first. Before any scene, generate the character on a plain background in the same light: front, three-quarter, profile and full body. Pick the best of each and keep them. They are the character now.
  2. Stills first, motion second. Make each shot as a still from the reference, then animate the still. Image-to-video usually holds a face better than text-to-video.
  3. Lock the wardrobe. One outfit per episode. A jacket that changes between shots is a continuity error unless the story asks for it.
  4. Regenerate, don’t patch. If a face drifts, go back to the reference and generate again. Adding words to rescue a bad take usually makes the next one worse.
  5. Name the files. CHAR_nia_front_v3.png, SH04_nia_door_take2.mp4. You will be glad at two in the morning.
  6. Two faces per scene. Every extra character in a shot is another face that can drift. Keep crowds out of focus or out of frame.

Part 04. The shot prompt library

One formula and 24 copy-ready shot prompts, sorted by the kind of shot.

The formula

[SHOT SIZE] of [CHARACTER: identity block] [ACTION: one verb], [LOCATION], [TIME + LIGHT], [LENS], [CAMERA MOVE], [MOOD + GRADE]

An image prompt describes the frame. A video prompt describes what changes in it: the action and the camera move. When you animate a still, don’t describe the whole picture again. Say what moves.

Swap anything in {braces}. Keep the light and the grade the same for every shot in a scene.

  3. P06

05

Part 05. Camera moves

The words video models respond to, what each move does, and when to use it.

A camera move is a sentence about how the audience should feel. Pick one per shot and name its speed.

MoveWhat the camera doesUse it for
Static (locked-off)Doesn’t move.Dialogue, tension, letting a face do the work.
Push in (dolly in)Moves toward the subject.Realisation, rising pressure.
Pull out (dolly out)Moves away from the subject.Reveals, isolation, endings.
PanTurns left or right on the spot.Following action, showing what’s beside.
TiltTurns up or down on the spot.Height, power, a slow reveal of a person.
Tracking (truck)Travels alongside the subject.Walking scenes, momentum.
Orbit (arc)Circles the subject.Big moments, decisions, reflection.
Crane (pedestal)Rises or drops.Opening or closing a scene.
HandheldSmall human shake.Panic, intimacy, a documentary feel.
Whip panA fast pan that blurs.Surprise, and transitions between shots.
Rack focusFocus shifts from one depth to another.Reveals, connecting two things.

The rules

  1. One move per shot. Two moves in one prompt usually gets you neither, or a camera that wanders.
  2. Name the speed. Slow, steady or fast. “Slow push in” and “fast push in” are different scenes.
  3. Lead with it if it’s ignored. If the model keeps dropping the move, put the move at the start of the prompt and try again.
  4. Dolly is not zoom. A zoom changes the lens. A dolly moves the camera, so the background shifts against the subject. Say “dolly” when you want depth to change.
  5. Static is a choice. The strongest shot in a scene is often the one where nothing moves except the person.

Part 06. Light and grade

Light words, the mood each one carries, and the grade words that hold a scene together.

LightReads as
Soft window lightCalm, natural, honest.
Hard top lightInterrogation, unease.
Practical lampsWarm, lived-in, late evening.
Phone or screen glowIsolation, late night, secrets.
Rim or back lightSeparation, drama, a person against the world.
Blue hourQuiet tension, something about to happen.
Golden hourNostalgia, warmth, a memory.
Sodium streetlightCity night with an orange cast.
Neon spillCity, mixed colour, energy or danger.
OvercastFlat, plain, sad.
Candle or firelightIntimate, flickering, old.
SilhouetteMystery, power, a stranger.

Grade words: muted, desaturated, teal and orange, lifted blacks, crushed blacks, warm film grain, cold blue, high contrast, pastel, bleach bypass.

Pick one light and one grade per scene, and put the same words in every shot prompt of that scene. Matching light sells continuity more than matching details do.

07

Part 07. Dialogue and voice

Writing lines that survive AI voice, lip-sync and a phone speaker.

  1. One line per shot. Short lines are easier to voice, easier to lip-sync and easier to follow on a phone.
  2. Write the subtext. People rarely say what they mean. “Did you eat?” can mean “I’m worried about you.”
  3. Read it out loud. If you run out of breath, the line is too long. Cut it.
  4. Direct the voice. Give every line a tone, a pace and a volume: “quiet, tired, slow.”
  5. Leave silence. A look after a line does more than a second line.
  6. Caption everything. Plenty of people watch with the sound off. The story has to survive it.

08

Part 08. The edit recipe

How to cut generated clips so they play as one scene.

  1. Assemble ugly. Lay every shot in beat order first, untrimmed. Watch it once for story only.
  2. Trim to the moment. Most generated clips have a short stretch in the middle where everything works. Cut the warm-up and the drift.
  3. Cut on movement. A cut during a turn of the head or a door swinging hides the seam between two generations.
  4. J and L cuts. Start the next shot’s sound before its picture (J), or let a shot’s sound run under the next picture (L). It is the cheapest way to make separate clips feel like one place.
  5. Four layers of sound. Room tone, foley (footsteps, keys, doors), dialogue, then score. Room tone under everything removes the dead silence that makes AI video feel like AI.
  6. Captions that read. Large, high contrast, two lines at most, in the middle of the frame. Keep text and faces away from the bottom and the right edge, where the app puts its buttons. Check it on your own phone before posting.
  7. Export for the phone. Vertical 9:16 at 1080 × 1920.
  8. End early. Cut before the audience is ready. That is what makes them watch it again.

Part 09. The hook bank

25 openings: ten for the dramas, fifteen for the posts about how you made them.

Replace {n} with your real number. Never round up.

Drama openings

  1. “This key isn’t mine.”
  2. Close-up of a text arriving: “Don’t come home tonight.”
  3. “Everyone on this block has a secret. Mine has a lock on it.”
  4. A door opens before anyone knocks.
  5. “I only lied once. It was enough.”
  6. A framed photo. One face has been scratched out.
  7. Two people under one umbrella. Neither is talking.
  8. “Who’s at the door?” “Nobody’s at the door.”
  9. A wedding ring left on an empty bus seat.
  10. “You weren’t supposed to see this.”

Behind the scenes

  1. I made this scene with no camera. Here’s the shot list.
  2. The prompt that finally kept her face the same.
  3. This took {n} generations. Here are the ones I threw away.
  4. Stop writing longer prompts. Do this instead.
  5. The cheapest trick in AI film is sound.
  6. One camera move per shot. Watch what happens when I break that rule.
  7. How I kept one character the same across {n} shots.
  8. Before I spend a single credit, I ask five questions.
  9. Same prompt, two lenses: 24mm and 85mm.
  10. Why your AI video feels like AI: the silence.
  11. Steal my shot list.

Part 10. The posting system

A weekly rhythm that turns one episode into a week of posts, and a four-week plan.

One episode a week is enough if every episode feeds the rest of the week. The episode is the show. Everything else is how you made it.

DayPostAsk
1The episode, 30 to 60 seconds.Nothing. Let it be a show.
3The breakdown reel: one technique from the episode.One line: comment a keyword for the pack.
5A carousel: the shot list or the prompts, made to be saved.One line: the workshop.
DailyStories: the process, a poll on the ending, the misses.Polls and replies, not links.
WeekEpisodeBreakdownCarousel
1Episode one.The five-question idea test.The beat sheet, filled in.
2Episode two.How the character stayed the same.The identity block and reference sheet.
3Episode three.One camera move, done three ways.Six shot prompts from the episode.

Post the misses. Rejected generations are content, and they are the proof that you are actually making things.

Part 12. The tool map

Pick tools by the job they do. Prompts move between tools; subscriptions don’t.

JobWhat to look forExamples to test
Character sheets and stillsImage references, consistent faces, control over framing.Midjourney, Higgsfield, Flux-based tools
Image to videoHolds a face from a still, follows camera moves.Kling, Veo, Runway, Higgsfield
VoiceDirection for tone and pace, a voice you can reuse.ElevenLabs
Music and scoreStems, or at least a clean ending.Suno
Edit and captionsJ and L cuts, caption styling, 9:16 export.CapCut, DaVinci Resolve

Tools, features and prices change every month, so treat the examples as a starting list, not a recommendation. Test any new tool on one shot from your current episode before you pay for a month of it.
